Rabbi Gene resumes his walk through the order of Nashim by taking up tractate Nedarim, vows — a subject that sits inside the Mishnah's section on women because, in the ancient world, a father or husband held the power to annul a woman's vow while a man needed a rabbinic court to release him from his own. Along the way he distinguishes a vow from an oath, traces the practice of court oaths on a holy book back to a thirteenth-century Spanish law code, and introduces the medieval Catalonian scholar known as the Ran, whose commentary on this tractate is said to outclass even Rashi's.

The teaching builds to Kol Nidre itself, explaining how Rabbeinu Tam reworked the prayer's language from annulling vows already made to annulling vows yet to come, and closes with the small phrase so many Jews still say without knowing where it comes from: bli neder, without making it a vow.

All right. I think holidays are over and I'd like to resume my little teaching sessions. And as it is a teaching session, if you have any questions, you can raise your hand. Or even at the kiddush afterwards, we could go into further discussion. Because what I'm going to talk about, I think, is a little arcane as far as most people are concerned. Remember, we were discussing the order of Nashim, which is in the Mishnah, one of the orders that is devoted only to women. And one of the reasons for that is that the rabbis understood that women in the society in which they lived were in an inferior position. And so laws had to be made in order to protect them. And we are quite aware that even though there are laws to protect women, abuse of women still continues, even in our own day and age, in all sectors of society.

So what does the tractate Nedarim, vows, have to do with women? Well, it's because women in those days went from reshut to reshut. They were in patria potestas. They were under the control of others, either their fathers or their husbands. And now we come to the question of vows. What is a vow? It's interesting, because you know that there are two tractates in the Mishnah that seem to be actually almost the same. There is a tractate of Shevuot, oaths, and a tractate of vows. And what's the difference between an oath and a vow?

Well, first of all, in most cases an oath may also entail taking the name of God. You know, it's interesting, in a court of law today you take an oath on a Bible and you swear to tell the truth, the whole truth and nothing but the truth, so help you God. You know that that first came into Western law in the 1200s, under Alfonso X, in his Siete Partidas, his law code. Alfonso lived from 1221 to 1284, and that was the first time in any law code in the West that oath-taking on a holy book was written in — but that was already part and parcel of Jews taking an oath. You took an oath either on your tefillin or on the Torah, and so that came into Western practice.

But an oath is where you take an obligation upon yourself to do such and such. Strangely enough, Jews are not allowed to swear to tell the truth, because you're taking God's name in vain. Why? Because mushba'im v'omdim meiHar Sinai. Jews are forsworn from Mount Sinai to tell the truth. Any Jew who doesn't tell the truth is actually taking God's name in vain, because they are sworn from Mount Sinai to tell the truth. However, there is an oath in Jewish law practice which is very interesting — an oath to tell all you know. That might work in some of the lawsuits that we have nowadays: somebody takes an oath and makes sure that you tell all that you know about a certain thing, and that could be applied.

Vows, on the other hand, a neder, are something that you abjure, that you will not do. In other words, if I will give up smoking for 30 days, or otherwise I'll give a hundred thousand dollars to synagogue — anybody wants to do that, we are willing to take that into consideration. In other words, a vow is where you are going to abstain from something, you're not going to do something, and that is a vow.

There's a very interesting book by none other than Saul Lieberman, who I had the great privilege of studying the tractate of Nedarim with, so many — over 50 years ago. What's interesting about the tractate, by the way, is that it's the one tractate where Rashi is outclassed by someone from Barcelona, Nissim ben Reuven, known as the Ran. He had a long life, he lived from 1290 to 1376, and he was the last of the great scholars of Catalonia. Most people don't understand, in the history, what happened in Spain before the expulsion. But there were many great scholars, especially in Catalonia, which is really a source of great Jewish commentaries and legal scholars — whether it be the Rashba. You know, they have, actually, in Barcelona, a street named for Solomon ben Adret, the Rashba, who was one of the first and the greatest of the scholars of that. And in this, the Ran's commentary is actually on tractate Nedarim.

So vows are quite common, and what Saul Lieberman says in Greek in Jewish Palestine is that people were vowing all the time, and the rabbis were trying to stop them. They were trying to stop them from taking vows. So it is better not to vow than to make a vow and then perhaps not do it. And so these were quite common at that time, and instead of saying that I will give an offering, a korban, they actually had these kinuyim, these sort of nicknames — konam, konam, if I don't do such and such. And so this whole tractate is devoted to vows, and what does one do when there is a vow and you can't do the vow?

You know, it's interesting that we see a woman — and this is why it's in the order which deals with women — a husband or a father could annul the vow of a woman. In other words, if a woman says, I'm going to — if I don't go to synagogue every morning, I will not serve my husband breakfast — well, right away the husband says no such thing. That was called hafarat nedarim. He's annulling the vow. On the other hand, if a man made a vow and wanted to have it annulled, he had to go to a rabbi or the beit din. And that was also called hafarat nedarim, in which the vows were cancelled.

We all know Kol Nidre, right? Kol Nidre is where most Jews come to synagogue, right? And what does Kol Nidre say? That all vows — the version that we have in the Ashkenazim says, from this day, Yom Kippurim haba aleinu l'tovah, from this time forward. That never was originally in the formulation of Kol Nidre. It was changed by Rabbeinu Tam, Rashi's grandson, who lived from 1100 to 1171. Rabbeinu Tam changed it because in the earlier version it said, it's from the Yom Kippur that was in the past to this Yom Kippur. In other words, it annuls vows as they were in the past. But according to Rabbeinu Tam, now it only annuls vows that are going to be made in the future.

What happens to the vows that you already made? Well, for that, Ashkenazi Jews found another way, another ceremony. There is something — I'm sure I'm saying something that nobody here knows — there's something called Hatarat Nedarim, which in my misbegotten youth I actually participated in. On the morning of Rosh Hashanah, in the old days, one had — the morning of Rosh Hashanah, you came to Selichot around six o'clock, and you had all these Selichot — you know, we only do Selichot now on the Saturday night before Rosh Hashanah, but every single day from that time on you had Selichot, and on Rosh Hashanah morning you had the longest Selichot in the world. You got up at six o'clock, and you had the main piyyut, it's from Rabbeinu Gershom, Me'or HaGolah.

In any case, after the service you had what was called Hatarat Nedarim. There were three people who would sit as judges, as a beit din, and the person would come and would say this long formula where they had charata, they had remorse that they had made the vow, and so the vow would be annulled. That word came into Yiddish — if you want to say you're sorry about something in Yiddish, you say, I have charata. Charata means I have remorse, and it comes from that particular way of annulling vows.

The influence of vow-taking actually has some residual things, if you are among the more, shall I say, observant community. If someone says, well, I'll see you tomorrow, bli neder, without making it a vow — I'll see you tomorrow, bli neder, im yirtzeh Hashem, if God willing — that is how, in the everyday speech of Jews, you say I'm going to do something, and you say bli neder, without making a vow. That's some of the influence of Nedarim, and that tractate is filled with many different folios, and I'm sure that it's something that I hope may interest you, but something that is part and parcel of our heritage.
