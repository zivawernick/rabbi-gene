On the Seventh Day of Passover, Rabbi Gene reaches back almost sixty years to
his seminary days, when he sat on the front row facing the ark between two
of the towering, and famously opposite, scholars of twentieth-century
Judaica: the Talmudist Saul Lieberman and Mordecai Kaplan, founder of the
Reconstructionist movement and author of Judaism as a Civilization. From
there he turns to a piyut by Yehuda HaLevi tucked into the Yom Tov Musaf
service — one Rabbi Gene wishes had never been cut from the siddur — and
shows how HaLevi wove the biblical story of Judah and Tamar into a
meditation on exile, identity, and the promise of redemption.

All right, I have to swallow the mic. It was going to be, well, soon enough,
almost sixty years since I graduated from the seminary, and I remember those
days so very, very fondly. One of the things that would happen is, whenever
I was there on a Shabbat morning, I would do the Pesukei D'Zimra, the
preliminary service. And of course I faced the ark in those days. On the
front row behind me were the greatest scholars of Judaica in the world. And
it was interesting, because they were quite a diverse group. On the one hand
you had Saul Lieberman, who was the greatest authority on Talmud. And on the
other side of the row — I don't know if they were even too friendly — was
Mordecai Kaplan.

Mordecai Kaplan founded the Reconstructionist Movement, although he himself
didn't really go along with where the Reconstructionist Movement ended up.
And one of the things — you have to be lucky, even when you publish a book —
Mordecai Kaplan's book, Judaism as a Civilization, which was one of the great
classics of scholarship, was published in 1939, right before World War II.
And that changed the direction of how at least the rabbis and the students
in the seminary looked upon things, because there was, as is typical in
Jewish history, a revanchement, a turning back to more scrupulous observance
because of the horrors of the Holocaust. And so Mordecai Kaplan's ideas in
Judaism as a Civilization really never caught on, and still have not caught
on, really. And the truth of the matter is that the Reconstructionist
Movement has devolved, or evolved — however you want to put it — into
something that I'm sure Mordecai Kaplan would not even recognize, or
certainly wouldn't like.

But the whole idea of Judaism as a Civilization is really something that
captured at least my mind. And I think one of the examples of that is that
even in our religious service we have interpolations such as this piyut,
which — unfortunately — is missing from our siddur. I don't know why. I
mean, maybe the people who put the siddur together had their reasons, and I
don't want to say anything amiss about them, but this is one of the great
examples of Jewish poetry.

And when we think in terms of the Jewish experience, it is a civilization.
It's not only what you would call a religion — that's a theological term
that doesn't really define us. We are a civilization. And as a civilization,
it's not only about being religious — fervent and observant — because
there's so much to be said about the Jewish experience. Sometimes I think
there are people who look at what constitutes the Jewish experience like the
blind man with the elephant: people grab onto one thing and miss the big
picture, because the big picture is that the Jewish experience is not only
religion, but poetry and literature. And one example of that is this
particular piyut.

And that's an interesting thing, because the very word piyut comes from the
Greek — it is a song, or poetry, that is interpolated into the religious
service. Isn't that amazing? I don't know of another so-called religion that
has this whole idea of literature and religious literature flowing from how
the people take what is our tradition and put it into their lives. This
particular piyut is known as a geulah, because it's placed into the last
blessing before the tefillah. If you look at it, the verses go down the
line, and you'll get the name of the writer: Yehuda HaLevi.

And that's one of the reasons why I was so upset that it was not included in
the siddur — because Yehuda ben Shmuel HaLevi is the greatest Hebrew poet of
all time. He's so very, very interesting, because he was a fervent Zionist.
During the morning service in a very Orthodox synagogue, there are certain
kinot, certain lamentations, that will be said — which, for whatever reason,
I guess they wanted to shorten the service, so they cut them out and
wouldn't have it. But one of them is his Zionides, his poems about Zion. His
most famous poem about Zion, Tzion halo tishali lishlom asirayich — Zion,
will you not ask for the welfare of those who are your captives — was taken
up by Russian Jews. Natan Sharansky — this was something that inspired them.

Because the life of Yehuda HaLevi is that he was born in Spain — they don't
even know exactly where. They think about 1075. He was born in what was
called Andalus, the Mohammedan, Muslim part of Spain, just as it was going
to undergo the Reconquista. And he left Spain and traveled on the way to
Israel, because one of his poems says, "I am in the West, but my heart is in
the East." There's an idea that he stopped in Damietta in Egypt, and they
wanted to detain him because he was so famous. And he wrote a poem — he
says, if you really do a favor for me, send me forth, and I will go forth to
my Lord. And the thought is that he was killed, either by a crusader or a
Muslim, on the road to Jerusalem.

And he was so interesting, because for all his great religious poems, he
also wrote wine songs — and some of them, by the way, keep your tzitzit on,
were homoerotic. It's very interesting; most people don't want to mention
that and don't even want to think about it. But he influenced people even
like Abraham ibn Ezra.

And I just want to give you a little bit of a sense of what he does with a
biblical story as he puts it into this particular piyut, which, by the way,
is filled with references that only, you know, if you really know your
stuff, you'd catch. I just want to show you line fifteen: Mi zot ne'eshemet
et hake'en ad davah emet, lami hachotemet vehapatilim. To whom does this
belong, that it can be recognized? Look now, see it as a thing of truth — to
whom is the seal and the cord?

Now, if you remember the story of Judah — after his sons Er and Onan die, he
doesn't want his son Shelah to marry Tamar. So he does nothing, and keeps
Shelah back. Tamar, therefore, dresses up — and this is very interesting —
Tamar dresses up as a prostitute, and Judah goes in to her. And as he
leaves, she says, well — what will you give me as payment? And she asks for
his seal and the cord that he has. And it so happens that she becomes
pregnant, and the child was going to be the ancestor of David.

Now, this is very interesting — the ancestor of David comes from an illicit
union. And when Judah finds out that she is pregnant, he says, well, she has
prostituted herself — take her out and let her be burned. So as she is being
led out to be burned, she shows them the seal and the cord, and he realizes
that he is guilty, not her. He is guilty, not her. And so she is saved, and
becomes the ancestor of David. This is an interesting story in itself. But
look what Yehuda HaLevi does with it. He says: look now and see what is
impressed, recognize now a word of truth. Lami hachotemet — now, the
chotemet here, he is referring to the brit milah, the covenant of
circumcision. Vehapatilim, the cords — the tzitzit.

And so he's saying to God: we are celebrating the going out from Egypt, but
we're still in exile. We're not in the land of Israel. And therefore, on
this holiday of Passover — especially on the seventh day, when we're talking
about the crossing of the Yam Suf, whatever body of water it was — we have a
history, and with it also a literature, which has enriched our lives and
turned our eyes toward a final redemption and restoration that has occurred
for the people in their land. Mi zot ne'eshemet, hake'en ad davah emet, lami
hachotemet vehapatilim. Page 155, Musaf al Kaddish.
