Rabbi Gene turns to one of the best-known aggadot in the Babylonian Talmud, tucked into the tractate on divorce, to explain how the rabbis accounted for the fall of Jerusalem to Rome in 70 CE. It is the story of a wedding invitation mistakenly sent to the wrong man, the public humiliation that follows, and a chain of small, legalistic choices that spirals into catastrophe. Though he is careful to note the story is moral fiction rather than history, Rabbi Gene draws from it a pointed lesson about public shame, the danger of prizing the letter of the law over its spirit, and the obligation to treat even one's enemies with basic human dignity — a lesson he extends candidly to Israel's own conduct in the present moment.

During these past weeks, I've been trying to bring some of the aggadah, some of the stories of the Talmud, whether from the Yerushalmi, the Talmud of the Land of Israel, or the Bavli, the Babylonian Talmud. And fortuitously enough, for this week, I chose one of the most famous stories, aggadah, that is found in the Babylonian Talmud, and is found in, of all places, the masechet, the tractate that deals with divorces. And I have to sort of give a prologue, that this is — as many of these aggadot are — made out of whole cloth. They are fictitious and should not be taken as historical. And what is so interesting is that so many different commentators try to put a historical sort of explanation and try to explain what the story is. But as I will endeavor to show, the story has a moral, like many of the aggadot, and it's not really that the fact that it's based upon fact, but the morality that it teaches, and the lessons that it teaches, are very, very poignant and interesting and important.

So what's the story? The story is, the rabbis are going to say, why was the temple destroyed? How did it happen that you could have such a foolish revolt against the Roman Empire, and that the Romans would come and destroy the temple and kill so many of the Jewish people in the year 70 of the common era? So this is the story that's found in the Babylonian Talmud. It's interesting that Rabbi Yochanan, who is mentioned at the end of the story, is the same Rabbi Yochanan from Tiberias, who is the mainstay of the Talmud of Eretz Yisrael. And in the Talmud of Eretz Yisrael, he happens to note that Jerusalem wasn't destroyed until there were twenty-four different sects and parties of Israelites, Jews, fighting with each other, which in itself is a lesson.

The story is that a man is making a feast. So he tells his steward to invite his close friend Kamtza. Now Kamtza, by the way, in Hebrew means somebody who's tight-fisted — komets is a fist. And the steward goes off and by mistake invites Bar Kamtza. Now it so happens that Bar Kamtza happens to be an eternal enemy of this fellow who's giving the feast. So Bar Kamtza comes anyway, since he's been invited, so he sits down at the feast. Everybody, all the big wigs from Jerusalem, are there, everybody's important, so he's sitting there, and then the host finds out who is sitting there — not his friend, but Bar Kamtza, the person he can't stand, who he sees as an eternal enemy. So he goes over and he says, what are you doing here? He's getting him out. So Bar Kamtza, of course, is going to be humiliated. So he says, listen, I'll pay you whatever I eat. He says, nothing doing. He says, I'll pay for half of the festivities. He says, nothing doing, I hate you. He says, I'll pay for everything. He says nothing, and he picks him up and throws him out.

So Bar Kamtza now has been humiliated in front of everybody — anybody who was anybody was there. And he says, how is it possible that nobody protested what was done to me? So he says, I'm going to get even with everybody. So he's going to go to the emperor — probably, in this story, Nero — and he says, you know, the Jews are revolting against you. And the emperor says, how should I know that? He says, I'll tell you what — and this is something that was done in temple times — they used to give a sacrifice in the name of the emperor. You know, of course, Nero was a bissel meshuga, and he wanted to be looked upon as a god. But instead, what the Jews did for the emperor, instead of worshiping the emperor as a god, they gave a sacrifice on behalf of the emperor. That's why even to this day, there's a prayer for the country in which you reside. I remember seeing prayer books, siddurim, from Russia, where there was a prayer for the Tsar. That wasn't only in Fiddler on the Roof — it was an actual prayer for the Tsar in prayer books printed in Russia.

So he says, all right, he says, I'll send the sacrifice with your hands, and you'll see, the Jews will sacrifice it in my honor. So what does he do? He puts a blemish in the animal — now there is a difference of opinion where he puts the blemish — but the main point of it is, he puts a blemish in the animal, which would make it a blemish according to the Jews, but according to the Romans, it wasn't a blemish. So there was no reason why, as far as the Roman emperor was concerned, it shouldn't be sacrificed. So now this Bar Kamtza comes to Jerusalem, and he's coming to the temple, and he has this animal which has a blemish.

So the rabbis don't know what to do. If you don't sacrifice the animal in honor of the emperor, you're liable to cause enmity, and the Romans will come, and there'll be war. So they say, well, maybe we should sacrifice it anyway. So Rabbi Zechariah ben Avkulas — one smart alec, always there — he says, you can't sacrifice a blemished animal. You can't put a blemished animal on the altar. So then they said, well, in that case, why don't we just kill Bar Kamtza, so he won't be going back to the emperor and telling him this? So Rabbi Zechariah ben Avkulas — and by the way, that's a very strange name, Avkulas is a Greek name, meaning he's very well bred, or whatever — eu kallos, kallos in Greek is beautiful — he says, you can't kill him. You don't kill somebody for just bringing a blemished animal. And so the animal is not sacrificed. The emperor is insulted, and the Romans come and destroy the temple and the Jewish state. And the same Rabbi Yochanan is quoted at the end, saying, because of the — shall we say — the modesty, or the probity and desire to keep the letter of the law, of Rabbi Zechariah ben Avkulas, our temple was destroyed and the Jewish people dispersed.

Now let's look at this story. First of all, it's not true. We know why the Jewish war against Rome developed and how it developed. Anyone who wants to read the actual facts can turn to Flavius Josephus, Yosef ben Matityahu HaKohen, called The Jewish War. I don't know — many years ago I got it in a Penguin edition. I don't know if it's still in the Penguin edition, but I remember reading it, and you could read the whole story as it actually happened according to Josephus, who, by the way, was a participant in that war.

So what is the story telling us? First of all, everybody stood by and saw this man insulted. And you know what's so interesting is that there's a rabbinic dictum — you know that anyone who insults, kol hamalbin pnei chaveiro berabim ein lo chelek le'olam haba, anyone who insults someone in public to the point that their face loses its color and they turn white, which, what Rabbi Nachman says, you see, it's like murder. Insulting somebody in public is like murder, because the blood rushes from their face, they turn white, and anyone who does that doesn't have a share in the world to come. Now what's the biggest thing that could happen to someone — kol hamalbin pnei chaveiro berabim ein lo chelek le'olam haba? And here he insulted him in public. How was it possible that everybody stood by, like he said? Everybody stood by and saw someone insulted? I guess you can put that to present day, where there are people who insult people in public. But the whole point is that that's one of the most horrible things that you can do. You know, one of the things that Judaism values is not only the physical aspect of a human being, but their reputation, how they look upon themselves. Belittling someone is a major sin. And here everybody was there, and they allowed this person to be insulted to the point where he was thrown out the door.

The second thing — he brings the sacrifice with this little blemish. And this one fellow, he's such a stickler for what's right, he says, well, you can't sacrifice, this animal has a blemish. Everybody says, but if we don't sacrifice it, we will cause the emperor to come and destroy us. That's beside the point. Now you see, there are some people who think in terms of the particulars to such a degree that they will sacrifice the forest for one tree. And an important point is also that — and he thought, he said, well, maybe we should kill him before he goes back and defames the whole people and brings about destruction. And here is Rabbi Yochanan — and at least it's said in Rabbi Yochanan's name — because you listened to this one fellow who was demanding that you stick to the letter of the law when the letter of the law is going to cause absolute destruction.

There are many different lessons to be taken from this little bit of aggadata. Now, naturally enough, none of this is real, but the lessons are real. The lessons are real, that there are times that you do not stick to the letter of the law in order for the greater good. And when you weigh the greater good over against the letter of the law, you go in favor of the greater good. And above all, you do not allow any person to be defamed, especially in public. Now you're supposed to rise up and value each and every single human being, no matter who they are — even if they are people who you don't like, even if they are people who are possibly enemies. One of the lessons that I think should be taught, that Israeli troops have to observe, even when dealing with Palestinians — I know that the horror of October 7th has hardened the hearts of many, but still and all, even those who are our most vicious enemies are still human beings who are not to be mistreated. And this is an important lesson that comes to this particular time of the year, when we think in terms of what caused the destruction of Jerusalem. And the rabbis are giving us a lesson, that only by strictly adhering to the principles of morality — not necessarily just the fine points of law, but the principles of morality — is what saves a nation, which, by the way, is what the Haftarah, the prophet Isaiah, said: Zion will be saved and redeemed by justice.
