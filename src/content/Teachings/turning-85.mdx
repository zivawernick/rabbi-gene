On the occasion of his eighty-fifth birthday, Rabbi Gene turns reflective, tracing decades spent working through the Talmud one tractate at a time. He shares a discovery he found while finishing Tractate Menachot: the little-known ancient Jewish temple in Leontopolis, Egypt, built by a displaced high priest during the upheavals of the Hellenization of Judea. From there he turns to a passage in the Tosafot that, read closely, offers a rare and quiet window into how Jewish communities in medieval Franco-Germany treated those who had converted to Christianity under duress — a subject, he notes, that the history books tend to pass over in silence.

I hope you will forgive me for at first being a little personal, but on Wednesday I had my 85th birthday, and one of the wonders of it is that I don't know how many years ago — it must have been over ten — my wife asked the oncologist that she needed twenty years, and he said two. So my very present standing here is a little bit of a blessing, at least for me. And one of the things that has always motivated my life is the rabbinic saying, "Yoter mishe'ha'egel rotzeh linok, ha-parah rotzah lehanik" — more than the calf wants to nurse, the cow has to give milk. And I feel like that cow, because I have been filled with so much in the way of study in all of these years through everything, that I really am very grateful that you seem to be a very patient congregation while I throw out all sorts of arcane stuff that no one else will tell you, because they don't know it. It is a sort of sorrow on my part — so many people who have the title rabbi actually know nothing. And I have always continued to study, and I study all sorts of things, which is wonderful, because you can find so many gems stuck away in the most unbelievable places.

And I have been going through the Mishnah with you on these Shabbat mornings — probably for two reasons, it's a double header. Number one, the Mishnah is the rock on which all of Jewish thought and religious thought and practice is based. It all goes back to that, to this particular corpus of Jewish practice. And that's what I like to always emphasize: halachah is not Jewish law. Law is something that you have in law codes. But halachah means how you live. It comes from the verb to walk. It's how you walk through life. And the rabbis try to explicate in every instance how you walk through life, from the time you get up to even how you sleep. Everything was examined.

And my son said, well, you know, these rabbis — I was going to speak about the fifth order of the Mishnah, which is called Kodashim, which deals with sacrifices. And in the Babylonian Talmud, there are tractates, masechtot, on these particular orders. Like you see, I give you this little sheet — Zevachim, which means animal sacrifices; Menachot, which means meal offerings. And my son asked me, he said, well, why did the rabbis in Babylonia — there was no temple, what were they doing? I said, well, they didn't have television. And so they were discussing what were the possibilities of these particular offerings. So of course there is also the third tractate in this particular order, known as Chullin, which tells you how to slaughter animals. I already finished that tractate, and now I know how to slaughter a chicken. I don't think that's going to be very practical.

But it's interesting, because you know, it goes through the whole way in which an animal should be sacrificed, slaughtered, and also afterwards — what most people don't know — that there is shechita, which they know is kosher slaughtering, but after the slaughtering there's something known as bedikah, and a bodek is someone who examines the animal to see if there are any blemishes in it, because that would make it not kosher. And in the United States of America, that's very easy, because in the big slaughterhouses, which are owned by non-Jews, they have a kosher section, and if there's any question as to whether the animal is not kosher or not, it doesn't cost anything — they just throw it into the non-kosher section, and that's very, very easy. But in Israel, every single part of the animal has to be used. There's no way of getting rid of it. And what most people don't understand, too, is that there is such a thing — in Hebrew, nikur, and in Yiddish, traybern — that the hindquarters of the animal are used in Israel, and they have people who actually extract the sciatic vein and nerve. And actually, that was done — I had an interesting childhood. In Washington Heights, there were some Lithuanian butchers who still did that. There was only certain butchers who were able to do that, and it was very interesting.

But that's not really what I want to talk about today. I'm taking advantage of it, because I just finished the Tractate Menachot, which deals with meal offerings in the Temple in Jerusalem, which is a very interesting thing, because what these rabbis were discussing was something that was purely arcane. But the thing is that when you go through these things, you get gems. At the very end of this Tractate Menachot — and I don't know if anyone has ever mentioned this — is a discussion of the Temple in Leontopolis. And I'm sure you don't even know about the Temple in Leontopolis, because it's a sort of out-of-the-way thing that happened.

It so happens that at the start of the Hellenization of the Jews under Antiochus IV in Israel, there was a high priest by the name of Chonyo, Onias, and he was replaced by his brother Jason, who was more of a Hellenizer than he was, and he bought his way from the king Antiochus, and Jason became the high priest, and he was a Hellenizer, and Chonyo went to Egypt and established a temple in Leontopolis. Now, how many people have ever heard of the Temple in Leontopolis? One person. All right — thank you, one person. And I mean, the Temple in Leontopolis is mentioned perhaps in Josephus, but in no place else. And what's interesting — and this is why it's so important to study — I mean, I'm now studying the Tractate Yoma. I mean, I'm going through every single tractate in the Talmud. I don't know anybody who has done that, that I know of.

But the end of Menachot, when it discusses this Temple in Leontopolis, it discusses what should happen to a Kohen who was in that temple and actually then went to the temple in Jerusalem. That temple, by the way, was destroyed in 73 by Titus of Aspation. But it said — so it's a moot point, but it said, what would happen if there's a Kohen who's officiating in the sanctuary — first of all, the very fact that there can be another sanctuary outside the sanctuary of Jerusalem is amazing, because you know, the rabbis decree that you're not allowed to sacrifice outside the temple. There was a rabbi, by the way, Matya ben Cheresh, who lived in Rome, who tried to sacrifice the Passover sacrifice in Rome, and the rabbis were against it.

So what happened is they said, what happens if this Kohen comes back to Jerusalem? So the rabbis there — and of course it's a moot point — say that he can't offer the sacrifices, but he can duchen. He can go up when the Kohanim go to the duchening — you know, when they bless the people, you've seen that perhaps from Orthodox synagogues, it's done in Israel all the time, where the Kohanim come up and they hold their hands like this over their head under the tallis, everybody looks the opposite way, and they say the threefold blessing.

Now, one of the other little shtiklach — you know, there's so many people that can't even read the Gemara, but what about the Tosafot? So the Tosafot on the side was also interesting. The Tosafot on the side said that a Kohen who had a blemish, even though he was away, could still come back and duchen. And I was thinking, you know, we think in terms of the Middle Ages, especially in Franco-Germany, and we think of all the terms of the martyrs — there's so many martyrologies — but nobody really discusses Jews who might have converted under force to Christianity. Remember that that happened in Spain — so many Jews, afraid for their lives, actually converted, the Marranos. And what about Franco-Germany, where there were all these terrible, or a default, ways in which Jews were either given the choice of death or actually converting? And what happens to them afterwards?

So this little Tosafot seems to imply something that is not really discussed. There were Jews who converted to Christianity. We know of one particular one who was the son of no other than the Meor HaGolah, Rabbeinu Gershom ben Yehuda — his son converted to Christianity. And he didn't sit shiva for him — he always wanted to think in terms that he would return, but he died even before that could happen.

So this is very interesting. It gives us an insight into something that is really not discussed. It's not mentioned in any history book that I know of, whether it's Graetz or Baron or Dubnow — that there must have been... everybody speaks in terms of all the martyrs, but what about all the Jews who converted, and what was the way in which they were treated? And this little note in the Tosafot, very silently, says these people are able to slip back into the community without being harangued for the fact that they didn't give up their lives instead of converting. And the anguish of those communities of Franco-Germany is all given in a little hint in the Tosafot, at the very end of the Tractate Menachot, which hardly, I think, anybody even considers looking at.

And so the study, even of these arcane things, you never know when you get a nugget, a treasure — and the treasure that is in all of these writings of the past, in the history of our people, sometimes hidden in languages which are hard for people to fathom and understand, and yet they contain insights into our past, and perhaps even lessons for our present, as we continue as a singular community, a family, a tribe, who has come from Sinai to this very day, suffering, I might say, the slings and arrows of outrageous fortune, and yet continuing in the one thing I think that distinguishes us from every other people: that Talmud Torah keneged kulam — the study of Torah is above everything else. It is that which maintains us even as we suffer persecution and even the threat of death, that the Jew must continue to study. And what does study do? Study gives us insight into how we should live — to live in peace and in blessing and in a sense of humility, as being human beings so subject to error that we recognize our own errors and are ready to suffer the errors of others graciously.

Rabbi Chananya ben Akashya said: God gifted the people Israel with the Torah, and the Torah is great and glorious. Page 155, we rise for Musaf Kaddish.
