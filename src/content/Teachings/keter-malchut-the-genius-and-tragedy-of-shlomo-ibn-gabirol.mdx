Rabbi Gene traces the roots of Hebrew poetry from its ancient Near Eastern
origins through the innovations of Golden Age Spain, arriving at the short,
brilliant, and largely forgotten life of Rabbi Shlomo ibn Gabirol. Known to
medieval Christian philosophers only by his Latinized name, Avicebron, Ibn
Gabirol's true identity as a Jewish poet was lost for centuries until a
nineteenth-century French scholar rediscovered it in a Paris library. Rabbi
Gene reads from Ibn Gabirol's masterwork, Keter Malchut ("The Crown of the
Kingdom"), a poem of philosophy and prayer that shaped medieval thought far
beyond the Jewish world.

I must confess that the reading from the Torah, from scripture, for this
morning is one of my favorites. And it is in poetry, which is an example of
biblical poetry going back thousands of years. I have often speculated that
people probably might have started singing before speaking — we know that
even birds have something like a song. And as soon as humans could learn how
to speak and put words together, there was this impulse for poetry. Poetry in
the ancient Near East goes all the way back to the Sumerians, to 3000 BCE.
There's an interesting book, if you read German, by Wolfram von Soden,
Sumerische Hymnen und Gebete, the Sumerian hymns and prayers.

In the Bible, in this particular Torah portion, the poetry exhibits what is
called parallelism, which curiously enough is also found in early Anglo-Saxon
poetry — which means that there are many ways in which this parallelism
appears. There is the simple parallelism, where the first part of the verse
is repeated in the second part of the verse. That could be supplemental.
Sometimes it's antithetical — the first part of the verse is actually negated
by the second part of the verse. And then there's an even more complex type
of parallelism known as chiasmus, from the Greek letter chi, which looks like
an X. In other words, it goes A1, B1, B2, A2, and this is found.

There has been — notice that there has been some rhyme in Hebrew poetry, but
it never really had any type of dominance. Rhyme, and actually Arabic meter,
was brought into Hebrew poetry in Spain by Dunash ibn Labrat, who actually
came from the Berber territories of Northern Africa. Dunash ibn Labrat, who
lived from about 920 to 990, was the first poet to also bring in the Arabic
meters, the Moush Wahkekin. There's one Arabic meter which actually imitates
the way a camel walks.

But the hero — or I should say one of the most unbelievably prolific and
magnificent poets of Spain, of the Golden Age of Spain, who is probably
really unknown — is Rabbi Shlomo Ibn Gabirol. Shlomo Ibn Gabirol lived a very
short life. He was born around 1020, and he figured he died sometime around
1050. He led a miserable life, but he was a rather arrogant fellow — there's
one poem in which he says, "I am the prince of the world. The poem is my
slave." And he said that was written when he was sixteen years of age. An
unbelievable genius.

And so I thought I would bring to you this, which is his most famous work,
the Keter Malchut, the Royal Crown. And what's interesting is that, under a
sort of transmogrification of his name, Ibn Gabirol was known in Christian
Europe as Avicebron, and was one of the great influences on the Neoplatonic
school of philosophy in the Middle Ages. And no one knew that he was really
Jewish until a scholar by the name of Salomon Munk, in 1846, in the National
Bibliothèque in Paris, figured out that Avicebron was none other than Shlomo
Ibn Gabirol.

I sort of thought I had given you a translation, but I see that what I gave
you in the English is not really a translation. So if you'll suffer me, I
will read the beginning of this very long poem, the crown of the kingdom.
Actually, it's not the Royal Crown, it's the Crown of the Kingdom.
