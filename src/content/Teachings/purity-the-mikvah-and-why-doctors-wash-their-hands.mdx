Rabbi Gene continues his walk through the six orders of the Mishnah by arriving at the one modern Jews find strangest: the laws of tumah and taharah, purity and impurity. Drawing on Claude Lévi-Strauss's observation that pre-modern peoples everywhere organized their world into realms of pure and impure, he shows this was never a uniquely Jewish idea — and then traces how one small piece of it, washing the hands before a blessing, quietly resurfaced centuries later in a Viennese hospital.

Along the way he walks through the ten grades of tumah, the custom of immersing new vessels in a mikvah before they can be used, and the reason a dining table is treated like an altar. It is a teaching about a subject most people assume is obsolete, delivered as a reminder of how much of it is still, in disguised form, all around us.
