Why does a line from the first chapter of tractate Brachot appear word for
word in the Passover Haggadah? Rabbi Gene works backward from Ben Zoma's
teaching on "all the days of your life" to a much older and juicier story:
the impeachment of Rabban Gamliel by the rabbis at Yavne, and the unlikely
teenager whose hair turned white overnight when he was chosen to replace him.
Along the way, the mishnah's promise — that redemption belongs to this world
and to the messianic age alike — becomes Passover's own promise of freedom
from slavery and from want.

For the lesson this morning, you'll see there's a sheet in front of you that
is actually the last mishnah in the first chapter of Brachot, and it is
repeated verbatim in the Passover Haggadah. I'm pretty sure that most people
don't even realize why it's repeated verbatim in the Haggadah, and you're
going to find out very, very soon.

The mishnah starts, Amar Rabi Elazar ben Azarya, harei ani k'ven shivim
shanah, velo zachiti sheteiamer yetziat Mitzrayim baleilot, ad shedarsha Ben
Zoma. Now, what you have to understand is that every single paragraph of the
Shema actually has a name, a separate name. The first paragraph of the Shema
is known as kabbalat ol malchut shamayim, the acceptance of the yoke of
heaven. The second paragraph is known as kabbalat ol mitzvot, the acceptance
of the yoke of the commandments. And the third paragraph of the Shema is
known as yetziat Mitzrayim. Even though it mentions tzitzit, why is it known
as yetziat Mitzrayim? Because it says in that particular paragraph, "I am the
Lord your God who brought you out of the land of Egypt." So that's why that
last paragraph is known as yetziat Mitzrayim.

So now the mishnah tells a very interesting story, and this is why I mention
it — because you're going to get the backstory. Rabbi Elazar ben Azarya says,
behold, I am like seventy years of age, and I did not have the merit of
knowing that we should say this third paragraph of the Shema at night,
because after all, the third paragraph deals with tzitzit, and tzitzit you
don't wear at night — you only wear it during the day. So why should we say
the third paragraph of the Shema at night? Until Ben Zoma — a pupil of Rabbi
Akiva — offered this special drasha. It's in the reading for the second day
of Shavuot, from chapter 16 of the book of Devarim: "In order that you should
remember your going out from the land of Egypt, all the days of your life."
So Ben Zoma says, is "the days" also "the nights"? All the days of your life
includes the nights. That shows that you should remember the going out from
Egypt all the days of your life, meaning even at night you should say it.

And they derive further: all the days of your life means in this world. All
the days comes to bring in the days of the Messiah as well. Now I will tell
you why this is found in the Passover Haggadah. What time of year does
Passover come? Spring. What other holiday, a non-Jewish holiday, happens in
the springtime? It's called Easter. And what is Christian theology? Once the
Messiah has come, you don't have to observe all the mitzvot. But what does
the rabbi say? To bring the days of the Messiah — even when the Messiah
comes, all the mitzvot will still be observed. And that's why it's in the
Passover Haggadah.

But now for the backstory. What does it mean when Rabbi Elazar ben Azarya
says, "behold, I am like seventy years of age"? This is a really juicy story.
It's found in the Babylonian tractate of Brachot, page 27a — you'll find the
right page. It's a long passage, and I'll make it brief. How many of you have
been in school and know there's always this smart-aleck kid who really wants
to make trouble? Well, that happened with the rabbis too.

It goes all the way back. After the destruction of the Temple, the rabbis
assembled at Yavne. Rabbi Elazar ben Azarya was there, but suddenly he
disappeared. And then a person by the name of Gamliel ben Shimon became the
head of the rabbis, the head of the religious court, the head of the
academy. He was known as Rabban Gamliel, Rabban Gamliel of Yavne. Even though
he was a great-grandson of Hillel, he wanted to enforce his authority — and
he enforced it so much that Rabbi Yehoshua ben Chananya, who was a disciple
of Hillel and a very gentle fellow (also a great astronomer), decided that
Yom Kippur was going to fall on a certain day. Rabban Gamliel said, no, it's
not going to be on that day — it's going to be on the day I say. And I want
you to come with your purse and your staff in front of me on the day you
think is Yom Kippur, to show that I'm the boss.

So what happens? There's a smart-aleck student. He goes to Rabbi Yehoshua ben
Chananya and says, listen — do we have to say the prayers in the evening?
Because, after all, the tefillot were instituted to correspond to the
sacrifices, and there were only sacrifices in the morning and the afternoon —
none in the evening. So Rabbi Yehoshua said, no, it's reshut — optional, you
don't have to. Then the smart-aleck student goes to Rabban Gamliel and asks,
do we have to say the evening prayers? Rabban Gamliel says, of course — it's
chovah, it's obligatory. So the student says, oh, is that so? I heard from
Rabbi Yehoshua ben Chananya that you don't have to. Rabban Gamliel says,
we'll see.

So the next day, he's in front of the whole academy, and Rabban Gamliel says,
is there anyone here who thinks you don't have to say the evening prayers?
Not a word from anybody. He says, Rabbi Yehoshua, stand on your feet — I have
heard it said that you disagree. Rabbi Yehoshua says, well, what can I do?
And he makes him stand the whole time while he keeps speaking. Now, here's
something you can use — I always like to tell you things that if there's a
lull in the conversation at a cocktail party, you can bring this up. All of a
sudden, all of the rabbis started to revolt. They said, what's the matter
with you, Rabban Gamliel? You've insulted this man so many times, and he's
so humble — we're going to remove you. So they removed Rabban Gamliel. This
was the first impeachment — a successful impeachment, I might say — that
ever was.

So then they say, well, who are they going to put in his place? They have to
find someone who has proper lineage. How about Rabbi Elazar ben Azarya, who
is like the tenth generation from Ezra? They say, yeah, but he's only a
youngster, a teenager — how can it happen? So, according to the legend, his
hair turned white overnight. And that's why he says, "behold, I am like
seventy years of age" — because, I guess, being brought up to a position of
authority, his hair turned white.

Well, the story actually has a happy ending. Rabban Gamliel, humbled, goes
and begs Rabbi Yehoshua ben Chananya to forgive him. Rabbi Yehoshua makes him
squirm a little bit, and finally they decide to restore Rabban Gamliel — but
they're not going to push Rabbi Elazar ben Azarya aside either. So three
weeks of the month Rabban Gamliel would be in charge, and one week would go
to Rabbi Elazar ben Azarya. And that's the backstory — the first revolt
against capricious authority. One of the things it shows is, number one, the
rabbis held to the whole idea that you don't insult another human being.
What Rabban Gamliel did went beyond that. And the interesting thing is that
he accepted the rebuke — that no matter who you are, no matter how great you
think you are, you should take the rebuke even of a common person. And so
Rabban Gamliel was restored to his office after he learned a lesson about not
wiping the floor with his fellow colleagues. Rabbi Elazar ben Azarya was
briefly elevated and gained respect, not only for his genealogy, but also
because he had a couple of great hairs to show for it.

And the story is found in the Babylonian Talmud, and also connects to this
reference which is found in the Haggadah shel Pesach, which refers to
yetziat Mitzrayim, the last paragraph of the Shema. And therefore he gave the
rabbis a lot to talk about. Page 155.
