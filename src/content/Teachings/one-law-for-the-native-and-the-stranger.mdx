Rabbi Gene takes up the Torah's repeated command to treat the stranger as an
equal, a theme he finds tucked into this week's reading: "there shall be one
law for the native and for the stranger who dwells among you." From there he
turns to xenophobia in its many guises — the demonization of "the other" in
the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, the rise of antisemitism and class
prejudice in America, and the ancient human instinct to fear what is
unfamiliar. He closes with the Seder's oldest invitation: "Let all who are
hungry come and eat."

It's a challenge to find something new to speak about, Shabbat after Shabbat
and holiday after holiday — sometimes it becomes difficult. But thank God for
the Torah — there's always something one can find that can spike your
interest, and, as the rabbis would say, it cries out darshuni, explicate what
it says. It's interesting that Passover, of course, is a holiday of freedom.
And Heinrich Heine said, wherever freedom is spoken of, it has a Hebrew
accent. And certainly the influence of Passover, the freedom of slaves, has
ignited interest not only among the Jews but around the world — especially if
one thinks in terms of some of the Black spirituals: Go down, Moses, way down
into Egypt land, tell old Pharaoh, let my people go. So freedom is something
that people across the world are interested in.

But what is so very interesting to me is that tucked away in this Torah
reading there is a line, Huqa Akhat, ti'e la Ezraach la Gaya Hagar
petohi'chem — there'll be one law, both for you, the native born, and the
stranger who dwells among you. One of the unfortunate facts of human life is
that, as Sholem Aleichem said, everybody has three sets of Shulchan Aruch,
the code of Jewish law. One for strangers, where everything is forbidden. One
for people you know, where some things are forbidden and some things are
not. And one for yourself, where everything is allowed. The unfortunate fact
is that we seem to allow anything for those who are closest to us.

And when we think in terms of strangers, we have what the Greeks call
xenophobia — fear of the stranger. We look upon anything that is strange, as
far as human beings are concerned, as potentially dangerous. And therefore we
greet other human beings with apprehension, sometimes fear, and sometimes,
unfortunately, with hatred and violence. I remember the story told in
Winnipeg of the two Jewish women who came to New York City and got into an
elevator with a very tall Black man with his German shepherd. And he said
"sit," and they sat on the elevator floor. I don't know if it's apocryphal or
not, but it really speaks to how we deal with people who we feel are
different. And it is a problem not only on the personal level, but also in
the way in which communities deal with each other.

One of the misfortunes of the conflict between Israelis and Palestinians is
the demonization of the other. For all the silly protests, it cannot be
forgotten that Hamas wishes to destroy every single Israeli Jew, and possibly
Jews outside the land of Israel. On the other hand, Israelis have responded,
sometimes too often, in their own ways that are far from kind. Twenty years
ago I opined — I said, how long will it take for Israelis to imitate the
people they're living among? Like Ben Gvir and Smotrich, and some of the
rabid people conducting horrible excesses against Palestinians in the West
Bank — they're actually just blending into the territory. That's how people
have lived in the Middle East and across the world.

It hasn't been sufficiently noted that there are no Jews left in Arab lands.
Once there was a thriving Jewish community in Iraq; they were chased out. The
community in Persia, whatever is left of it, lives in terror — one of the
leaders of the Jewish community was publicly hanged from a construction
device. The amount of hatred, just because someone is different. And the way
in which the Torah tells us to deal with other people is to see them as
possible friends and members of the community. Freedom does not mean the
right to use excess against other human beings who do not belong to your
group.

And it has to be said that even in these United States of America, there is
too much prejudice. Of course, we're seeing a rise of antisemitism. I wonder
about these protest groups, whether they're really interested in the people
in Gaza, or much more interested in making life miserable for Jews in the
United States of America. And that goes even to the so-called halls of
learning. One of the prejudices that people have is that if somebody has a
college degree, it makes them into a better human being. And that's a
prejudice, of course, that is found in our land too. The type of alienation
that exists in this country between those who live in the heartland and
those who live on the coasts is because we no longer see each other as
individuals, or even grant that others may have certain virtues that may
even be lacking among us. Just because someone has a college degree does not
make them necessarily an ethical, moral person. To prove my point, all I have
to say is Joseph Goebbels had a PhD. It did not improve the moral quality of
his life.

The ability to see people as people, as equal, and entitled by right to the
same particular rights and privileges that we have — that is what freedom
means: that we live by a law in which all are equal. We have not become free
from slavery in order to enslave others. And the challenge of Passover is
contained in the very beginning of the Seder, in those Aramaic words: Ha
lachma anya di achalu avhatana b'ara d'Mitzrayim. This is the bread of
affliction which our ancestors ate in the land of Egypt. We should know what
it means to suffer — and knowing what it means to suffer gives us not the
right or the privilege to do that to others, but the burden of spreading the
message of Passover: that all human beings should be free.

And freedom also means freedom from want. It is a disgrace that in the
United States of America, somebody has to go on television and ask for
charity so that children should be fed. How unbelievable it is that there
are those who are more interested in children who are unborn than in
children who are born. Ha lachma anya — this is the bread of affliction. Kol
dichfin yeitei v'yeichol — anyone who is hungry, anyone who is famished, let
them come and eat. The whole idea is that every single human being is
entitled to meals. One of the things that the rabbis established, when the
Jews lived in the land, is the tamchui, a soup kitchen that provided for
every single person in the land, regardless of who they were — two meals a
day, and three meals on Shabbat, so that no one should go hungry. What a
disgrace it is that in this country anyone should go hungry when we can ship
wheat across the world.

And it goes on: v'chol dichfin yeitei v'yifsach — and all those who are
suffering oppression, all those who are being put upon by others, let them
come and celebrate the Passover, which carries a promise of freedom for all
human beings. And so in the Seder we open the door for Elijah, because we
speak not only of the present but of the future. Judaism is a religion that
believes in the future of this world, a world that can support all of its
creatures abundantly, because the earth is beautiful. Very soon we will
celebrate it with the blessing for tal, for dew — the dews that fall and the
rains that come, for every single human being, and for every single human
being to realize the worth of each other and the right of each and every
individual on this planet to live in security. Too often so many wars have
been fought, so many lives have been lost, so many people are left bereft,
and yet the lesson is not learned. But the voice goes forth: Kol dodi — the
voice of my beloved is knocking. Every single Passover we are reminded that
the voice is calling, it's knocking at our door. The answer is waiting for
all of us to open the door. We continue now, page 155 for the Musaf Kaddish.
Then we're going to go to page 123, where we have the beginning of the
prayer for tal.
