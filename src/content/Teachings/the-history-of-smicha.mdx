Rabbi Gene traces the surprising history of rabbinic ordination — from the
three-tiered smicha established by Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi in Roman-era Israel,
through its abolition in the fifth century, to its unlikely reinvention in
sixteenth-century Safed by Rav Yaakov Berav, who ordained none other than
Joseph Karo. Along the way, he paints a picture of daily life and Jewish
self-governance in the land of Israel, from compulsory schooling to the
rival academies of Babylonia, and closes with the newly excavated mosaics of
the Galilean synagogues.

Alrighty, I'd like to continue talking about the Mishnah — not only because
historically it is the source of all Jewish practice, but also because it
gives us an insight into the life of the Jewish people in the land of
Israel. We are combating so much ignorance. There was a continuous presence
of Jews in the land of Israel from the time they first came in to the
present day. It was with difficulty at times that they survived there,
because there was constant pressure from others trying to drive them out.
But the real, I would say, golden period of Jewish life in the land of
Israel was from the time after the destruction of the Second Temple, and
even after the failed Bar Kochba revolt of 135, because Jewish life was
reconstituted, and from the same line as Hillel there were leaders of the
Jewish High Court who directed how life was to be led in the various
hamlets of the land of Israel.

Just recently, in National Geographic, there was a whole section on the
discovery of a synagogue in Hukuk in northern Israel, where Jewish life
really thrived after the Bar Kochba revolt — with beautiful mosaics that
come from this synagogue. Anybody who's been to Israel, at least to Beit
Alpha and other places, has seen the beautiful synagogues that were erected
throughout the land of Israel. This is in the period from, let's say, the
100s to the 400s or 500s, when the Byzantine Christian Empire managed to see
to it that a great majority of Jews left the land. But still there was a
minority, and right up until the time of the War for Independence, there was
a plurality of Jews in Jerusalem — 40% of the population was Jewish, with
smaller percentages Muslim and Christian. If you remember the Old City,
there were these different quarters, and there was the Jewish Quarter, which
was of course next to the Wall.

But how were the people who lived in this land governed? We're talking now
about the Mishnah, and the fourth order of the Mishnah, known as Nezikin,
and the first three tractates in it, known by the gates — Bava Kamma, Bava
Metzia, Bava Batra — show how the rabbis adopted and adapted the laws in the
Torah for life in the period in which they were living. As a matter of fact,
the last of those three tractates, at the beginning of Bava Batra, deals
with the laws of partnership, which are not found in the Torah at all. So
much for people who are originalists. The law actually guides, or follows,
how human development goes, both commercially and industrially and
otherwise, in order to find ways for people to live.

Now the patriarch, the Nasi, such as Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi — I'll give you a
peg: we're talking about the year 200 of the common era. I like to date the
redaction of the Mishnah to 212 of the common era, which fits with the laws
of Antoninus Caracalla, but in any case, let's say about 200 of the common
era for a peg. The patriarch Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi saw to it that there were
schools throughout every single little townlet in Israel. In other words,
every single boy was given an education — almost always a compulsory
education for minor boys. Unfortunately not for girls, but that was the
first instance I know of compulsory education. In every single place there
were teachers appointed, and also rabbis who would be decisors as to how
people should live.

Now, one of the interesting things that was done was something known as
smicha. Smicha, if you follow the Bible, is from when you place your hands
on a sin offering and transfer your presence, your essence, to the offering,
which then stands in your place. But the smicha that was instituted by the
Nasi gave the person who received it — the rabbi — permission to adjudicate
different parts of Jewish halacha. And remember, halacha is not only law,
but also how you live. It was divided by Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi into three
parts. There was Yoreh Yoreh, which gave the person who received that type
of ordination the right to decide such things as whether an animal was
kosher or not, or how one conducts prayer. There was a higher degree known
as Yadin Yadin — "he will judge" — which made the person capable of
presiding over things such as divorces. Only a person who had the smicha of
Yadin Yadin could grant a divorce or adjudicate matters of damages, because
remember, in the land of Israel you could collect damages, and the
compensation covered all sorts of injury done to a human being. And then
finally there was a third stage, known as Yatir Bechorot, which meant that
you could declare that a certain firstborn animal was no longer fit to be a
sacrifice on the altar.

What was very interesting is that the academies in Babylonia — when they
were established by a person known as Abba Arikha, known as Rav, who
established the academy of Sura that existed down to the year 1000, or the
academy of Nehardea, which was made by Shmuel, which — by the way, was
destroyed, as I've mentioned before — and moved to Pumbedita, so the two
great academies of Babylonia were Sura and Pumbedita. And by this time the
Jewish population of Babylonia was greater than the Jewish population of
Eretz Yisrael. But when Rav — both Rav and Shmuel were pupils of Rabbi
Yehuda HaNasi — when Rav went to Babylonia, he asked Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi for
the smicha: Yoreh Yoreh, Yadin Yadin, Yatir Bechorot. He didn't give him the
permission — he held back, which is very interesting, and you should forgive
a sort of apikoros way of looking at it: Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi did not wish
the academies of Babylonia to rival the academies of Israel, which they
could easily do, because they had more pupils and very great scholars. And
that actually continued even into the decisions of the Talmud, because when
Rabbi Yochanan bar Nafcha, who was from northern Israel, disagreed with Rav
and Shmuel, the halacha always goes according to Rabbi Yochanan, who came
from Eretz Yisrael, not from either Rav or Shmuel or anyone from Babylonia.

Now, when the Nesiut, the patriarchate, went away in the 400s, there was no
more smicha — no more ordinations for rabbis. So how is it that rabbis are
ordained today? Well, you can thank a fellow by the name of Rav Yaakov
Berav, who in 1538 in Safed decided to reinstitute the whole idea of smicha,
and gave ordination to none other than Joseph Karo. He reinstituted the
whole idea of smicha, and so now, any Orthodox rabbi, when he gets his
ordination, his ordination is Yoreh Yoreh — in other words, he is given the
permission to decide whether something is kosher or not. The whole idea of
Yadin Yadin is an extra-special type of ordination that is only given to
certain really learned Orthodox rabbis, and even today — mostly in Brooklyn,
as well as in Eretz Yisrael — there are rabbinical courts where, when people
have a particular conflict over something that is a monetary case, they
would go to a din Torah.

Actually, the seminary from which I graduated, the Jewish Theological
Seminary of America, also confers ordination, but it's certainly not the
type of ordination given to Orthodox rabbis who study the laws of kashrut

Rabbinical Assembly, and there are people who do go to the beit din at the

the things the beit din at the seminary did do is that the Rabbinical

marriages — something rather new — because when a husband refuses to give a
Jewish divorce to his wife, and he is recalcitrant, she can apply to the
court of the Rabbinical Assembly, and they grant an annulment of her former
marriage.

But all of this goes to prove that during this whole period in which Jews
lived in the land of Israel — from after the destruction of the Temple,
after the Bar Kochba revolt, down through the 300s and 400s — they had an
established community with a school system and a legal system by which they
were judged. They lived in peace, and most of the time in harmony, and built
