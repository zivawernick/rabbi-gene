The full implications of the early rabbinic period that produced the ultimate guide for Jewish life (The Mishnah) has never been sufficiently explored. The reason is that the Babylonian Talmud because of the continuance of Jewish life in what was Babylonia continued well into the eleventh century. The inheritors of that Talmud's authority continued as it became the source of all religious decisions accepted by both the Ashkenazim of Europe and the Sephardim of lands of the Moslem conquest. In contrast the Jewish communities of Israel by the fifth century of the Common Era were diminished and the Talmud of the Land of Israel, commonly known by the misnomer the Yerushalmi, was never edited and subsequently neglected in religious rulings by commentators and codifiers.

It is to be noted that both Christianity and Islam are based upon an acceptance of Biblical theology with the importance of miracles and miraculous occurrences that defy reason. The Judaism of the second century of the common era that was formulated and controlled by the rabbis who constructed the Mishnah was influenced by Greek philosophy. Rabban Shimeon ben Gamliel is quoted as reporting that under the aegis of his father Rabban Gamliel half of the students in his academy were studying "Greek Wisdom." (B.T., *Sot.* 49b; Tosefta, *Sot.* 15.8)

Of little note is B.T. Avodah Zarah 54b in which a dialogue between certain "philosophers" and rabbis was held in Rome. It must have happened on a trip to Rome featuring among them Rabban Gamliel in an effort to influence the Roman government in favor of the Jewish settlement in Israel. The dialogue had to be in Greek because it is highly doubtful that the "philosophers" knew either Hebrew or Aramaic. It also assumes that whoever the "elders" were they had to be sufficiently able to conduct a dialogue in Greek!

The "philosophers" challenged the rabbis as to the obvious permission that the Jewish "God" gave to the worship of idols. The rabbis responded with what is perhaps the first statement of "Natural Law:" —"the world acts according to its custom." The fall-back that sinners would be punished in the future was based on the near universal belief of practically everyone in that era believed in a "life" after death.

It was Rabban Gamliel who had the daily prayers set in order (TB Brakhot 28b) and fixed them thrice daily (TB Brakhot 27b).

In keeping with the restriction of miracles and miraculous occurrences the daily prayer has as its eighteenth prayer the expression of miracles as the daily wonders in which the world is gifted:

> We are thankful to You that You Adonoy are our God and the God of our fathers forever; Rock of our lives, You are the Shield of our deliverance in every generation. We will give thanks to You and recount Your praise, for our lives which are committed into Your hand, and for our souls which are entrusted to You, and **for Your miracles of every day with us, and for Your wonders and benefactions at all times— evening, morning and noon**. (You are) The Beneficent One— for Your compassion is never withheld; And (You are) the Merciful One— for Your kindliness never ceases; we have always placed our hope in You.
>
> (Siddur Ashkenaz Sefaria).

The desire to restrict the miracles of the Bible to being part of the natural order of the world is seen in an anonymous Mishnah in chapter five, eight in Mishnah Avot:

> Ten things were created on the eve of the Sabbath (of creation), at twilight:
>
> The mouth of the earth (Numbers 16), the mouth of the well (Numbers 12:16), the speech of the ass (Numbers 22), the rainbow (Genesis 9:13), the manna, the rod (Moses's use thereof for miracles Exodus 4:17), the Shamir (legendary worm that could eat the hardest stone), the script (writing on the Tablets of ten commandments), the writing instrument (with which the letters were written on the Ten Commandments), the tablets.

Clearly what could not be denied as in written text was to be restricted to past events that were not part of the natural order of the world in which we live!!!!

So too: Mishnah Brakhot 9:3

> One who cries (to change) what is past, behold this is a vain prayer.
>
> How is this the case?
>
> His wife is pregnant and he says may it be Your will that my wife should give birth to a boy; Behold this is a vain prayer. He is coming back on the road and he hears a crying in the city and he says may it be Your will that these should not be in my house; Behold this is a vain prayer.

The person who is the foundation of all the important texts from the period of the Mishnah is none other than Rabbi Akiva:

> As Rabbi Yochanan said: the Mishnah is Rabbi Meir, the Tosefta is Rabbi Nechemiah, the Sifra (Commentary on Leviticus) is Rabbi Yehudah, the Sifrei (Commentary on Numbers and Deuteronomy) is Rabbi Shimon and all of them are according to the school of Rabbi Akiva. (TB Sanhedrin 86a).

This is supported by a text from the "Yerushalmi Talmud": "Rabbi Yonah said:...this is Rabbi Akiva who set in order the Midrash (commentary) of law and legend." (TJ, Shek. 5:1, 48c)

Rabbi Akiva by his own admission was late in life becoming part of the rabbinic class (TB Pesachim 49b). This does not mean he was ignorant but one may guess that he was influenced by Greek language and learning. The Mishnah of his pupil Meir is the only early rabbinic compendium that shows an arrangement in accord with the logic developed by Aristotle and other Greek sages. The early designation of the "rabbis" as "Hachamim-the wise" is equivalent to the Greek Sophists and the Wisdom cherished by the rabbis is nothing other than "Sophia."

Of Rabbi Akiva's students none was the equal of Rabbi Meir. Meir was reputed to be a convert. (Mishneh Torah, Transmission of the Oral Law 9, Sefaria). An anonymous Mishnah accepted as law is ascribed to Rabbi Meir and the reason for its anonymity is related in a statement in (TB Hor. 13b-14a).

The approach to the gentile world was before the destruction of the Second temple typified by Hillel, the founder of the most important rabbinic school:

> Hillel used to say: be of the disciples of Aaron, loving peace and pursuing peace, loving mankind and drawing them close to the Torah. (Mishnah Avot 1:12).

This is borne out in a statement attributed to Jesus:

> "Woe to you Scribes and Pharisees, you hypocrites! You travel over land and sea to win a single convert...." (Matthew 23:15)

Rabbi Meir is quoted as saying that a gentile who studies Torah is like a High Priest. (TB Bava Kamma 38a).

Rabbi Yehoshua ben Hananya was of the opinion that all the righteous gentiles of the world have a share in "the world to come." (BT Sanhedrin 105a)

In the Tosefta Chagigah 2:2 and later in the Babylonian Talmud Chagigah 14b there is reference to four rabbis who went "up to Pardes." This is a legendary account and there is no reason to believe that the four together went to "Pardes." But it is reasonable enough to believe that the term "Pardes" does not refer to something other than a "Greek Academy." The "groves of Academe" were the place of Plato's philosophical discussions.

Note the similar usage found so many places in the Talmud for "the vineyard in Yavne" as the place for rabbinical discussion. See inter alia: TB Shabbat 33b, 138b; Tosefta Eduyot 1:1 (Other places listed in Sefaria).

Towards proof thereof of the four Rabbi Elisha ben Avuyah became a follower of the Greek way of life casting off all obligations of rabbinic practice.

Rabbi Akiva used his knowledge of Aristotelian logic to be the origin of the only rabbinic literature that follows a logical pattern the "Mishnah." It is an accident of history that the Babylonian community and its Talmud survived past the Palestinian experience centuries after the Jewish community in Palestine lost population and prestige.

Of the remaining two who went up to "Pardes" Shimon Ben Zoma and Shimon Ben Azzai both taught under the influence of Greek wisdom.

> Ben Zoma said: Who is wise? He who learns from everyman, as it is said: "From all who taught me Have I gained understanding. (Psalms 119:99) (Mishnah Avot 4:1)

Ben Azzai is quoted: For one commandment leads to another commandment, and transgression leads to another transgression. For the reward for performing a commandment is another commandment and the reward for committing a transgression is a transgression.

> He used to say: do not despise any man (term used for anyone Jew or non-Jew), and do not discriminate against anything, for there is no man that has not his hour, and there is no thing that has not its place. (Mishnah Avot 4:2,3)

It is not a stretch to believe that the universal attitude of these two sages is unique for any time and place. Ben Azzai's teaching emphasizes not a reward in a "heaven or hell" but who you are and what you wish to become.

How does one square the rigid system of conduct prescribed in the Mishnah with the philosophy of these sages? Note that the inclusion of Ben Zoma and Ben Azzai in Mishnah Avot immortalized them as this Tractate of the Mishnah is read by pious Jews one chapter every week between Passover and Rosh Hashanah!

In my mind the Mishnah is a prescription for house-breaking human beings (the apex predator) so they do not do harm to themselves and others. The edifice the rabbis created mandated how one was to act from the time they awoke to even when they were asleep.

As in Mishnah Bava Kamma 2:6:

> A person is responsible for the damage he(she) causes, whether awake or asleep, and must pay full damages.

It was the misfortune of the times that the dominant Talmud was to be that of Babylonia with its emphasis on weird tales, devils, incantations and wild stories.

It is to be noted that even a Kabbalist and anti-Maimonidean rationalism as the Ramban (Rabbi Moses ben Nachman-Nachmonides) in his disputation of 1263 (the only Jewish-Christian disputation won by a Jew!) discarded the Aggadta (stories) of the Babylonian Talmud as not having any theological meaning for Jews!

The calamities of the Iberian Jewish community, the massacres of 1391 and the subsequent mass conversions, the expulsions from Spain and Portugal of 1492 and 1497 with their subsequent shockwaves throughout the Jewish world buried the humanistic philosophy of the teachers of the Mishnah with the ascendency Kabbalah and increased scrupulousness in the performance of ritual.

It was not until the nineteenth century and the revival of Jewish interest in rigorous scholarship with the "Wissenschaft Des Judentums" in Germany that a more enlightened attitude to Jewish prayer and practice was to become ascendant.

Unfortunately, the Holocaust has had the same effect as the misfortunes of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. The unlucky timing of Mordecai Kaplan's "Judaism as a Civilization" to be published in 1933 has marred its influence.

The same dangers of annihilation faced by Jews in Israel living under the pressure of constant combat has also impacted the philosophy and practice of Judaism in the place where it all began.

Despite it all, Jews owe to themselves and the world in which we live to preach and practice the outlook of the sages of the Mishnah who knew, practiced and preached that only as we take full responsibility for all of our actions and see the world and all that it contains as a "Holy Place" is there hope for a future for humanity and all who inhabit this planet.

As the rabbis added to the reading of Avot by every Jew between Passover and Rosh Hashanah, ascribed to Rabbi Meir (student of Rabbi Akiva):

> "Everything that the Holy One Blessed Be He created in this world, He created only for His glory, as it is said: "Everything that is called by My name, I created it, I formed it, I made it for My glory" (Isaiah 43:7) (Mishnah Avot 6:11)
